# HIGH LEVEL SUMMARY

## Summary
This paper presents CARE (Calibrated, Attribution-Rewarded rEservation), a proactive resource reservation controller for network-sliced 5G handovers that explicitly trades off handover protection against the displacement of incumbent users in the target cell. The framework combines post-hoc logistic regression calibration of trajectory forecast descriptors, per-contract counterfactual credit assignment with operator-priced incumbent displacement ($\lambda$), and an optimal-stopping formulation solved via an ensemble of three neural Fitted Q Iteration (FQI) networks. Evaluated in a synthetic system-level simulator across 20 held-out paired seeds, CARE substantially raises handover admission (from 51.6% to 87.4%) and adapts its reservation rate to the displacement price, while the authors transparently demonstrate that a simple calibrated threshold rule tuned on separate seeds matches or slightly outperforms the learned controller and diagnose why on-policy policy-gradient learners (PPO) collapse on this task.

## Strengths
* **Principled Domain Primitives and Problem Formulation:** Translating trajectory forecast descriptors into a calibrated target-cell entry probability (reducing the Brier score from 0.083 to 0.044) and pairing it with floor-protected just-in-time activation and explicit incumbent displacement pricing provides a clean, practical formulation for per-UE handover reservation. Ablations confirm that probability calibration is the single largest contributor to handover admission performance (+3.22 percentage points).
* **Rigorous Experimental Design and Scientific Transparency:** The evaluation uses strictly disjoint seed partitions across forecaster training, calibration, RL training, rule tuning, and testing, paired comparisons with Holm-Bonferroni multiplicity corrections, and honest reporting that a well-tuned calibrated threshold rule matches or slightly outperforms the FQI controller across all displacement prices.
* **Insightful Diagnostic Analysis of Policy-Gradient Collapse:** Formulating handover reservation as an optimal-stopping problem via off-policy Fitted Q Iteration effectively avoids the degenerate all-or-nothing policy collapse exhibited by on-policy Monte Carlo policy-gradient methods (PPO), offering a valuable methodological diagnostic for sequential stopping tasks in networking.

## Weaknesses
* **Omitted Mathematical Definitions and Unresolved Cross-References:** Several core display equations—specifically the per-contract counterfactual reward $R_j$, the system-level net utility $J_\lambda = U_\lambda$, and the load-dependent latency and handover QoS indicators—are missing from Sections IV-B and V-B, leaving unresolved `(??)` cross-references throughout the text and hindering self-contained mathematical reproducibility.
* **Replay-Buffer Non-Stationarity Under Multi-UE Capacity Coupling:** While Section VI-C claims that a committed contract's realized reward $R_j$ is independent of the behavior policy and can be reused indefinitely across all 240 training episodes, concurrent reservation decisions by other UEs directly alter the target cell's total active holds $h_c$ and remaining free capacity $F_c$ as $\epsilon$ anneals from 1.0 to 0.05; please consider clarifying this multi-agent off-policy distribution shift in the FQI formulation.
* **Residual Action-Value Overestimation and Missing Algorithmic/Domain Baselines:** Even with a three-network ensemble, CARE underperforms the tuned threshold rules at every price and still commits costly holds on 2.0% of decisions at $\lambda = 16$; the authors might consider evaluating Double Q-learning updates as used in PreNS or offline Conservative Q-Learning to suppress overestimation bias, as well as comparing against classical cell-level guard pools or continuous probability-weighted shadow-cluster reservations to isolate the value of discrete per-UE contract management (Reference Paper: PreNS: A Hybrid Predictive and Real-Time Resource Allocation Framework for 5G and Beyond RAN Network Slicing, B. Wu et al., 2026; Reference Paper: Offline and Distributional Reinforcement Learning for Radio Resource Management, E. Eldeeb and H. Alves, 2025; Reference Paper: Predictive and Adaptive Bandwidth Reservation for Hand-Offs in QoS-Sensitive Cellular Networks, S. Choi and K. G. Shin, 1998).
* **Simplified Synthetic Evaluation and Fixed Nearest-Cell Target Selection:** Because the simulator relies on a 4-cell square topology with 2D Gaussian random-walk mobility, a memoryless priority scheduler required for exact one-step counterfactuals, and fixed nearest-site target selection, the sequential decision problem reduces to a nearly one-dimensional threshold on calibrated confidence $p$ where deep RL offers no net advantage over tuned heuristics (and generalizes slightly worse when preparation delay increases without retraining).

### [CARE Methodology, Learning Algorithms & Experimental Setup (Sections VI–VII)] (Pages: 5-7)
#### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

1. **Potential Non-Stationarity of Realized Contract Rewards ($R_j$) Under Multi-UE Coupling (Page 5, Section VI-C & Algorithm 1):**
   In **Section VI-C (Page 5)**, to justify accumulating and reusing every logged transition across all $E = 240$ training episodes in an unbounded replay buffer $\mathcal{D}$ without eviction or importance weighting, the text states: *"A committed contract's target is its realized counterfactual reward, which does not depend on the behavior policy, so every transition can be reused."* While committing a hold terminates the stopping decision sequence for that individual UE crossing (per Constraint (10), eliminating future single-UE continuation bootstrap bias $Q_{\bar{\theta}}(s', x')$), asserting that the realized reward $R_j$ is strictly independent of the behavior policy is a potential theoretical and methodological concern in this shared-capacity multi-UE system. Because up to $N = 32$ concurrent UEs share cell capacity $C = 60\text{ RBs}$ and act under the same $\epsilon$-greedy behavior policy (Algorithm 1, Lines 6–10), the behavior policy's propensity to commit holds alters the aggregate active reservation $h_c = H_c(t)$ and pending holds $P_c(t)$ at target cell $c$. Specifically:
   - **Activation-time feasibility (Constraint (7)):** Higher concurrent reservation rates by other UEs increase $H_c(t_j^a)$, changing the likelihood that contract $j$'s activation is rejected when scheduled to activate.
   - **Counterfactual admission rescue (Eq. (1)):** Whether the arriving UE would have failed or succeeded without contract $j$ depends on the remaining unreserved capacity $F_c = C - \sum a_u - H_c$ left by other UEs' active holds.
   - **Proportional displacement cost (Eq. (5)):** The displacement charged to contract $j$, $D_j = \frac{b_j}{h_c}\min(h_c, u_c)$, depends nonlinearly on the total active reservation $h_c$ placed by concurrent UEs whenever $h_c > u_c$.

   Because Algorithm 1 anneals $\epsilon$ from $1.0$ (where 9 of the 10 actions are holds, causing frequent concurrent reservations) down to $0.05$ over the first $180$ episodes while retaining all transitions in $\mathcal{D}$ from Episode 0 onward, early transitions in $\mathcal{D}$ reflect higher target-cell contention and a different distribution of $R_j$ than later episodes. It would strengthen Section VI-C to clarify that $R_j$ is free of *intra-UE continuation bootstrap bias* (consistent with the single-UE decoupling approximation of Section V-D), while acknowledging that cross-UE capacity coupling introduces mild reward non-stationarity across the replay buffer as $\epsilon$ anneals.

2. **Confounding Factors and Formulation Choices in the Policy-Gradient (PPO) Failure Diagnosis (Page 6, Section VI-D & Table III; Page 6, Section VII-C):**
   **Section VI-D (Page 6)** and **Section VII-C** evaluate two PPO variants (`CARE-PPO w/ on-policy wait` and `CARE-PPO`) to explain why policy-gradient learners collapse to all-or-nothing policies on this optimal-stopping task. Three aspects of this comparison represent potential methodological concerns that warrant clarification:
   - **Sampled-return flooring vs. value-critic bootstrapping:** Section VI-D notes that under on-policy Monte Carlo returns, a `WAIT` action inherits the return of the UE's next sampled decision, and that *"Flooring the sampled continuation at 0 removes the collapse but overestimates waiting, because a single lucky rescue dominates the sampled maximum."* Applying the nonlinear operator $\max(0, G_{t+1})$ directly to a single noisy trajectory's Monte Carlo return $G_{t+1}$ introduces positive Jensen's inequality bias ($\mathbb{E}[\max(0, G_{t+1})] \ge \max(0, \mathbb{E}[G_{t+1}])$). In standard actor-critic PPO implementations (e.g., with a learned state-value baseline $V_\phi(s)$ and Generalized Advantage Estimation), optimal-stopping continuations bootstrap from the expected value critic $\max(0, V_\phi(s'))$ rather than a raw single-path return.
   - **Action-space skew in multi-step exploration:** Under the on-policy wait backup at high displacement prices ($\lambda \in \{4, 16\}$), holding yields strongly negative returns ($-18.73$ at $\lambda = 16$ in Table VI) whereas waiting until arrival without holding yields $0$. The collapse to holding occurs because the 9-to-1 ratio of hold-to-wait actions in the discrete action space makes independent per-step exploration across lead windows $k \in \{1, \dots, 8\}$ almost surely trigger a hold before arrival ($1 - \Pr(\text{WAIT})^k = 1 - (1/10)^k$ initially) unless the initial policy or action prior is weighted toward waiting.
   - **Training budget disparity:** According to **Table III (Page 6)**, CARE (FQI) is trained for $E = 240$ episodes per replica, whereas the PPO ablation is trained for only $160$ episodes (one-third fewer environment interactions, alongside omitting PPO's learning rate, minibatch size, and network architecture from Table III). Clarifying whether a value critic $V_\phi(s)$ was used, framing the collapse specifically around unbaselined Monte Carlo continuation backups and the 9-to-1 action ratio, and explaining or equalizing the 160- vs. 240-episode budget would make this diagnosis more rigorous.

2. **Statistical Interpretation of the 44-Test Holm-Adjusted $p$-Values as "Statistical Indistinguishability"**
   - **Location:** Page 7, Section VII-D (left column), Section VIII-B (right column, second paragraph), and Section VIII-C (right column, fourth paragraph).
   - **Issue:** In Section VIII-B, the paired differences in $U_1$ between CARE ($12.38$) and `Floor-JIT` ($12.56$), `Tuned rule` ($12.59$), and `Slice-tuned rule` ($12.65$) are reported as `-0.18 [−0.29, −0.07]`, `-0.21 [−0.34, −0.08]`, and `-0.27 [−0.43, −0.11]`, with Holm-adjusted $p$-values of $0.0741$, $0.0705$, and $0.0529$ across a predeclared 44-test family (alongside a $-0.09$ percentage-point difference in whole-network full-service rate against the slice-tuned rule with adjusted $p = 0.00169$). The text concludes from this that *"CARE thus learns a policy whose network outcome is statistically indistinguishable from, and at worst marginally below, a calibrated threshold rule tuned on separate seeds."* This phrasing raises two potential statistical interpretation concerns:
     1. *Power reduction under a 44-test multiplicity correction when arguing indistinguishability:* Controlling the family-wise Type I error rate across all 44 heterogeneous tests simultaneously (7 baselines and 4 ablations across 4 metrics) applies a strict multiplicity penalty (dividing the $\alpha = 0.05$ threshold by roughly $25\text{–}35$ for these ranks). While a large family is conservative when claiming superiority, failing to reject the null hypothesis after a 44-test step-down correction does not establish statistical equivalence, especially when the unadjusted 95% confidence intervals for all three rule comparisons on $U_1$ lie strictly below zero (unadjusted paired $p \approx 0.0015\text{–}0.003$).
     2. *Significant adjusted $p$-value on full-service rate:* Because $p = 0.00169$ on whole-network full-service rate (as well as $p = 0.00824$ on $U_{16}$ in Section VIII-C) remains statistically significant at $\alpha = 0.05$ even after Holm adjustment, describing the overall network outcome as "statistically indistinguishable" is slightly imprecise.
   - **Suggestion:** Refine the phrasing in Section VIII-B to state that the paired differences in $U_1$, HSR, and outage against the tuned rules are small in magnitude and do not remain statistically significant after 44-test family-wise error correction (while noting that the unadjusted confidence intervals for $U_1$ exclude zero), and describe the statistically significant difference on whole-network full-service rate ($-0.09$ percentage points) as practically negligible.

3. **Endogeneity of the Per-Decision Hold Share Metric, Non-Monotonicity Across $\lambda$, and Slice Breakdown Clarity**
   - **Location:** Page 7, Section VIII-C (right column); Page 8, Table VI (right column); Page 9, Section VIII-E and Fig. 8.
   - **Issue:** Several potential concerns affect how action-commitment percentages and price-response gaps are reported and interpreted:
     1. *Denominator expansion and non-monotonicity of per-decision hold share:* Section VIII-C states that as the displacement price rises from $\lambda = 0.25$ to $\lambda = 16$, *"The share of its decisions that commit a hold falls from 23.7% to 2.0%."* However, Table VI shows that the per-decision hold share does not decrease monotonically: between $\lambda = 0.25$ and $\lambda = 1$, CARE's hold share rises from `24%` (`23.7%`) to `26%`, and `CARE single network` rises from `49%` to `65%`. Moreover, because a decision point $(i, t)$ only occurs while UE $i$ has *no open contract* (Section V-A and Constraint 10), choosing `WAIT` at interval $t$ generates an additional decision point at $t + 1$ for the same cell crossing, whereas committing a hold immediately removes the UE from future decision points for that crossing. Thus, waiting expands the denominator (total decision points): at $\lambda = 0.25$, Section VIII-C notes that CARE creates `275.4` reservation contracts per run compared to `437.9` for the tuned rule (which holds on 100% of decisions), meaning CARE actually commits a contract on roughly 63% of handover forecasts ($275.4 / 437.9$) after waiting 2–3 steps, even though its per-interval decision hold rate is `23.7%`.
     2. *Reference baseline for price-response gaps:* Section VIII-C states that CARE's *"utility stays within 0.6 RB per interval of the best tuned rule at every price. The gaps (CARE minus tuned rule) are -0.59, -0.21, -0.05 and -0.25 at $\lambda = 0.25, 1, 4, 16$."* Those four numbers correspond specifically to `CARE` minus `Tuned rule` in Table VI; however, at $\lambda = 1$ and $\lambda = 4$, the *best* tuned rule (bolded in Table VI) is `Slice-tuned rule` (`12.65` and `6.78`), for which the gaps are `-0.27` and `-0.33` (still well within the stated $0.6\text{ RB}$ per interval bound).
     3. *Slice ordering and unconditional vs. conditional percentages in Section VIII-E:* Section VIII-E states at $\lambda = 1$ that *"CARE mostly waits (Fig. 8): it waits on 71.6% of uRLLC, 64.8% of eMBB and 82.4% of mMTC decisions.    - **Suggestion:** In Section VIII-C and Table VI, briefly explain that waiting expands the number of decision points per crossing (and why the per-decision hold share increases between $\lambda = 0.25$ and $\lambda = 1$, or report the per-crossing contract commitment rate alongside the per-decision share), and clarify that the listed gaps (`-0.59, -0.21, -0.05, -0.25`) are computed against `Tuned rule` (whereas gaps against `Slice-tuned rule` are `-0.43, -0.27, -0.33, -0.25`). 4. **Ambiguous Terminology and Incomplete Metric Reporting for the Contract-Mechanics Ablations**
   - **Location:** Page 8, Section VIII-D (*"Contract mechanics (Floor-JIT, $\lambda = 1$)"*, bottom of right column) and Page 9 (top of left column).
   - **Issue:** Two potential clarity and completeness concerns arise in the contract-mechanics ablation discussion:
     1. *"Unprotected" vs. "floor-protected" activation terminology:* Section VIII-D states: *"With unprotected activation, 48.9% of activations are rejected and admission falls to 60.6%: the floor-protection rule claims the safe capacity instead of racing incumbents for it."* Under Constraint (7) in Section V-C, floor-protected activation allows a hold to claim capacity up to $C - H_c(t_j^a) - \sum_u f_u(t_j^a)$ (i.e., preempting incumbent allocations above their minimum slice floor $f_u$). By contrast, what Section VII-C and Section VIII-D call *"free (unprotected) activation"* restricts activation to strictly unallocated RBs $F_c = C - H_c(t) - \sum_u a_u(t)$ after incumbents' *full* demands $a_u(t)$ are served. Referring to this stricter free-RB-only rule as "unprotected activation" can confuse readers because it protects incumbents' full allocations rather than only their admission floors, which is why 48.9% of hold activations are rejected.
     2. *Omitted primary metrics:* Unlike the four learner/calibration ablations reported across all seven metrics in Table V, the four contract-mechanics ablations (no retiming, free-RB-only activation, one decision per interval, and simplified mechanics) are summarized only by point estimates of handover admission rate (HSR) in prose, without reporting their displacement, outage, full-service rate, net utility $U_1$, or confidence intervals.
   - **Suggestion:** Clarify in Section VIII-D that "free (unprotected) activation" restricts hold activation to leftover unallocated RBs ($F_c$) rather than claiming capacity above incumbents' admission floors, and consider including the contract-mechanics ablations in Table V (or a compact secondary table) with full metrics ($U_1$, Outage, Displacement).

5. **Clarifying the Mechanism Behind Sensitivity to Preparation Delay ($\delta_p = 3$)**
   - **Location:** Page 9, Section VIII-F (*"Sensitivity without retraining"*, bottom left and top right columns) and Page 10, Fig. 9 (right panel).
   - **Issue:** Section VIII-F notes that at a three-interval preparation delay ($\delta_p = 3$), CARE's admission rate drops to 79.4% against 85.8% for the tuned rule *"because its learned timing assumes that holds activate one interval after the request."* Under the contract mechanics in Sections IV-C and V-A, both CARE and the tuned rule predominantly select `floor/jit` ($\delta = k - 1$), which schedules activation at $t_j^a = t + \max(\delta, \delta_p)$ and retimes as forecasts update.    - **Suggestion:** Explicitly link the degradation at $\delta_p = 3$ in Section VIII-F to CARE's shorter commitment lead (5.22 vs. 7.30 intervals).
