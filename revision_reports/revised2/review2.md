# HIGH LEVEL SUMMARY
---
## Summary
This paper presents CARE (Calibrated, Attribution-Rewarded rEservation), a proactive resource reservation controller for network-sliced 5G radio access network (RAN) handovers. To balance handover protection against the displacement of incumbent traffic, CARE combines a post-hoc logistic calibrator that maps observable mobility forecast descriptors to target-cell arrival probabilities, a per-contract counterfactual credit assignment mechanism with budget-balanced incumbent displacement pricing, and a discrete-time optimal-stopping formulation solved via Fitted Q Iteration (FQI) over a three-network ensemble. Evaluated in a synthetic 4-cell, 32-UE system-level simulator across three network slices and 20 paired held-out seeds, CARE substantially improves handover admission and reduces outage compared to unreserved and reactive baselines, while the authors transparently demonstrate that a simple calibrated confidence threshold rule matches or slightly outperforms the learned FQI controller across all displacement prices and diagnose why on-policy PPO collapses into all-or-nothing policies.

## Strengths
* The per-contract counterfactual credit assignment and budget-balanced displacement pricing mechanism is a well-motivated contribution to RAN reward engineering, preventing credit inflation in underloaded cells and enabling operators to explicitly control the trade-off between handover admission and incumbent service degradation via a single price parameter.
* Integrating post-hoc probability calibration over kinematic and geometric forecast descriptors to guide individual, floor-protected just-in-time handover contracts is practically impactful, with ablations demonstrating that target confidence calibration is the single largest learning contributor to handover admission gains.
* The experimental methodology and scientific transparency are commendable, featuring strictly disjoint seed partitions, Holm-corrected paired randomization tests, an insightful diagnostic analysis of why standard on-policy policy gradients fail under asymmetric stopping action spaces, and candid reporting that a well-tuned calibrated threshold heuristic matches or slightly outperforms the complex FQI learner.

## Weaknesses
* Because the simulator relies on noise-free Euclidean Voronoi cell boundaries, a 2D random walk, and a constant 99% radio draw rather than 3GPP Event A3 (hysteresis and time-to-trigger) handovers under realistic shadowing and fading, the reservation decision reduces to a nearly one-dimensional function of calibrated confidence where the learned FQI policy fails to outperform a simple calibrated threshold heuristic (Reference Paper: Predictive and Adaptive Bandwidth Reservation for Hand-Offs in QoS-Sensitive Cellular Networks, Sunghyun Choi and Kang G. Shin, 1998; Reference Paper: ECHO: Enhanced Conditional Handover Boosted by Trajectory Prediction, Anna Prado, Hansini Vijayaraghavan, and Wolfgang Kellerer, 2021).
* The authors might consider evaluating training-time bias-reduction techniques—such as Double Q-learning targets or conservative offline updates— alongside sliding-window replay buffer management, as retaining early exploration-heavy transitions with inflated cross-UE contention and relying solely on inference-time ensemble averaging leaves single-replica maximization bias in the `WAIT` bootstrap targets that contributes to CARE trailing the tuned threshold rules (Reference Paper: PreNS: A Hybrid Predictive and Real-Time Resource Allocation Framework for 5G and Beyond RAN Network Slicing, B. Wu, P. C. Amogh, N. V. Abhishek, and M. Gurusamy, 2026; Reference Paper: Neural Fitted Q Iteration – First Experiences with a Data Efficient Neural Reinforcement Learning Method, Martin Riedmiller, 2005).
* Please consider expanding the empirical comparison to include probability-scaled pooled reservation schemes under the same displacement price, as well as clarifies in the counterfactual credit model how the asymmetry between leave-one-contract-out rescue credit and proportional displacement cost-sharing behaves when multiple holds simultaneously contend at a target cell (Reference Paper: A Resource Estimation and Call Admission Algorithm for Wireless Multimedia Networks Using the Shadow Cluster Concept, David A. Levine, Ian F. Akyildiz, and Mahmoud Naghshineh, 1997).
* In the policy-gradient diagnostic ablations, the observed all-or-nothing collapse is confounded by an unfactored 9:1 hold-to-wait action-space initialization prior and single-sample Jensen's inequality bias in the zero-floored Monte Carlo return, which the authors might want to disentangle by testing a hierarchical binary stopping head or critic-bootstrapped continuation targets.

## Potential Issues And Suggestions
### [Framing, Related Work, Problem Scenario, and References] (Pages: 1-3, 11)
#### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

1. **Scope of Baseline Comparisons and Justification for Omitting Pooled Reservation Schemes (Section II, Page 2):**
   In Section II (*Relation to PreNS*), the manuscript explains that slice-level allocation frameworks such as PreNS [8], classical cell-level guard pools [5], and probability-scaled shadow-cluster reservations [6], [7] are excluded from the empirical baselines because they allocate slice- or cell-level capacity pools rather than per-UE reservation contracts, and that restricting all baselines to share CARE's forecaster, calibrator, and contract mechanics isolates the per-handover stopping policy. While this design choice cleanly isolates the decision policy within the per-UE contract paradigm, grouping pooled handover reservation schemes ([5]–[7]) with general slice-share allocation (PreNS [8]) as answering "different questions" is a potential concern for evaluating the broader architectural thesis of per-UE contracts:
   - Unlike PreNS (which allocates aggregate slice-level capacity across all traffic), cell-level guard pools and probability-scaled shadow-cluster schemes address the exact same operational problem as CARE: holding target-cell resource blocks ahead of predicted handovers to prevent admission blocking at the cost of displacing unmet incumbent demand $u_c$.
   - Under the simulator's priority scheduler and Proposition 1, the total incumbent displacement at cell $c$ in any interval depends strictly on the aggregate active hold $h_c = \sum_{j \in \mathcal{A}_c(t)} b_j$ via $\min(h_c, u_c)$. When multiple UEs are simultaneously forecast to cross into the same target cell within overlapping lead windows with calibrated confidences $p_i < 1$, isolated per-UE contracts reserve capacity additively for each committed UE, whereas a probability-scaled pooled scheme (e.g., sizing a shared target-cell or per-slice pool proportional to expected arriving floor demand $\sum_i p_i \hat{f}_i$) can exploit statistical multiplexing across uncertain arrivals under the exact same aggregate displacement price $\lambda$.
   - Given the empirical finding in Section VIII that simple calibrated threshold rules (`Tuned rule` and `Floor-JIT`) match or slightly outperform the learned FQI controller within the per-UE contract mechanics, either including a simple probability-scaled pooled reservation baseline under the same displacement pricing objective $J_\lambda$, or expanding Section II to explicitly distinguish slice-share allocation from pooled handover reservation and discuss the lack of statistical multiplexing in per-UE contracts, would strengthen the positioning of the contract-based formulation.

2. **Contextualization of Headline Performance Claims and Trade-offs in the Abstract and Introduction (Pages 1–2):**
   A potential concern in the Abstract and Section I is that several headline empirical figures are presented without specifying the baseline, pricing regime, or underlying service trade-offs to which they apply:
   - **Baseline and Price Context for Admission and Outage Gains:** The Abstract and Introduction state that *"CARE raises handover admission from 51.6 to 87.4 percent and cuts outage from 5.42 to 1.42 percent of device-intervals."* As shown in Table V (Section VIII-B), these numbers reflect the comparison against the `No reservation` baseline at a single displacement price ($\lambda = 1$), whereas the `Reactive` reservation baseline already raises admission to 83.5% and reduces outage to 1.87% (leaving a $+3.91$ percentage point admission gain and a $-0.45$ percentage point outage reduction for CARE over reactive reservation). Explicitly noting in the Abstract and Introduction that the 51.6% $\rightarrow$ 87.4% improvement is relative to `No reservation` at $\lambda = 1$ (and briefly citing the marginal gain over `Reactive` reservation) would provide clearer upfront context.
   - **Handover Admission vs. Full-Demand Handover QoS Trade-off:** The Abstract and Contribution 2 (Section I) highlight that CARE credits reservations both for rescued admissions and for rescued full-demand QoS outcomes. However, because the reward function (Section V-B) weights a full-demand QoS rescue at $q = 4$ RB compared to $(1 + T_o)d_j$ RB for an admission rescue ($42$ RB for a mean eMBB arrival), CARE and the tuned threshold rules almost exclusively commit minimum admission-floor holds (`floor/jit`). Consequently, while CARE improves the handover admission rate by $+3.91$ percentage points over `Reactive` reservation (87.4% vs. 83.5%), its full-demand handover QoS rate is $14.5$ percentage points lower than `Reactive` (51.7% vs. 66.2% overall, and 50.9% vs. 65.4% for uRLLC) and $15.3$ percentage points lower than `Fixed proactive` (67.0%). Although Section VIII-B explains this trade-off transparently, briefly noting in the Abstract or Introduction that CARE achieves its admission gains and low incumbent displacement (0.92%) primarily by reserving slice admission floors—trading full-demand handover QoS for higher admission reliability relative to full-demand reactive holds—would prevent readers from inferring from the opening sections that both admission and handover QoS rates improve simultaneously over reactive reservation.
   - **Non-Monotonicity of Per-Decision Commitment Shares Across Prices:** The Abstract states that *"As the displacement price rises, CARE holds less often, from 23.7 to 2.0 percent of decisions."* While this holds between the endpoints ($\lambda = 0.25$ and $\lambda = 16$), Table VI (Page 9) shows that the per-decision commitment share is not strictly monotonic across intermediate prices: it increases from 23.7% (rounded to 24% in Table VI) at $\lambda = 0.25$ to 26% at $\lambda = 1$, before dropping to 20% at $\lambda = 4$ and 2.0% at $\lambda = 16$ (because waiting creates subsequent decision points for the same crossing and enlarges the denominator, as explained in Section VIII-C). Rephrasing the Abstract slightly to specify that the per-decision hold share drops from 23.7% to 2.0% between the lowest and highest displacement prices would ensure strict consistency with Table VI.

3. **Incomplete Characterization of the Activation-Timing Trade-off in the Problem Scenario (Section III and Fig. 1, Page 3):**
   When motivating why arrival-time uncertainty makes the timing decision (`wait` vs. holding `now`, at `mid`-lead, or `jit`) nontrivial, Section III states only one side of the trade-off: *"The arrival time is uncertain, so a hold activated too early withholds capacity from incumbents for longer than necessary."* Mentioning only the penalty of early activation does not explain why a controller would ever commit or activate early rather than always waiting until the last possible interval before the forecasted arrival. Under the contract mechanics formalized in Sections IV-C and V-C (and demonstrated empirically in the preparation-delay sensitivity analysis in Section VIII-F), delaying commitment or activation carries two opposing risks: (1) if the UE arrives earlier than the forecasted lead $k$ while the contract is still in its preparation delay $\delta_p$ or prior to $t^a$, the hold is not yet active and provides zero reserved RBs during the admission test; and (2) because Constraint (9) checks incumbent admission-floor isolation at the activation instant $t_j^a$ rather than at request time $t$, delaying activation risks activation rejection if incumbent load at the target cell rises before $t_j^a$. Briefly stating both sides of this timing trade-off in Section III would make the conceptual problem scenario self-contained and better aligned with the formal contract mechanics.

### [System Model and Mathematical Formulation] (Pages: 3-5)
#### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

1. **Inactivity of the Modeled Latency Condition Under the Full-Demand Requirement and Ambiguity in Counterfactual QoS Rescue (Section IV-B, Equation 2 and Table II, Page 3):**
   In Section IV-B, a handover meets QoS if *"it is admitted, receives its full demand, and its modeled latency*
   $$\ell = \ell_s \left( 1 + 0.75 \rho_c^2 + 3 \left( 1 - \frac{\min(d, m + F_c)}{d} \right) \right)$$
   *is within the slice limit of Table II,"* where $\ell_s \in \{8, 2, 22\}\text{ ms}$ is the base latency of $(\text{eMBB}, \text{uRLLC}, \text{mMTC})$ and the corresponding slice latency limits in Table II are $\{20, 5, 100\}\text{ ms}$. Two potential concerns arise from this specification:
   - *Inactive latency gate under the full-demand prerequisite:* Because meeting QoS already requires the UE to receive its full demand ($\min(d, m + F_c) = d$), the fractional shortfall penalty $3\left(1 - \frac{\min(d, m + F_c)}{d}\right)$ in Equation (2) is identically zero for every handover that satisfies the full-demand requirement. Furthermore, because the target-cell load satisfies $\rho_c = \left(\sum_{u \in \mathcal{U}_c} a_u + h_c\right)/C \le 1$ by Constraint (8), the maximum possible modeled latency for any full-demand handover is $1.75\,\ell_s$, which evaluates to $\{14, 3.5, 38.5\}\text{ ms}$—strictly below the Table II limits of $\{20, 5, 100\}\text{ ms}$ for every slice. Consequently, as written, the latency formula in Equation (2) and the slice latency thresholds in Table II can never be violated when full demand is met, rendering the latency check inactive for both actual QoS and counterfactual QoS rescue ($\mathbf{1}_j^{\text{qos}}$). It is worth clarifying whether QoS satisfaction was intended to be gated by Equation (2) without a separate strict full-demand condition (allowing the shortfall penalty to gate partial allocations), or whether $\ell$ is evaluated independently elsewhere.
   - *Counterfactual target-cell load $\rho_c^{\text{cf}}$ and treatment of held vs. free RBs:* Immediately below Equation (2), the text defines counterfactual admission rescue using $F_c^{\text{cf}} = F_c + \max(0, m - u_c)$ and states that *"QoS rescue is defined the same way."* However, removing the arriving UE's active hold of $m$ RBs also changes the target-cell load $\rho_c$: active holds decrease from $h_c$ to $h_c - m$, while total incumbent allocations $\sum_{u \in \mathcal{U}_c} a_u$ increase by $\min(m, u_c)$ as the memoryless scheduler hands released RBs to unmet incumbents, yielding
     $$\rho_c^{\text{cf}} = \frac{\sum_{u \in \mathcal{U}_c} a_u + \min(m, u_c) + (h_c - m)}{C} = \rho_c - \frac{\max(0, m - u_c)}{C} \le \rho_c.$$
     Moreover, because $\rho_c$ includes the arriving UE's pre-allocated hold $m$ inside $h_c$ before handover settlement whereas unreserved free RBs from $F_c$ consumed upon arrival are excluded from $\rho_c = (C - F_c)/C$, holding $m > u_c$ RBs increases $\rho_c$ (and thus increases $\ell$) compared to an unreserved arrival that claims the exact same RBs from $F_c$ at handover time. Explicitly writing out the counterfactual latency expression $\ell^{\text{cf}}$ and clarifying whether $\rho_c$ is updated to $\rho_c^{\text{cf}}$ or held fixed in the counterfactual QoS check would make the evaluation of $\mathbf{1}_j^{\text{qos}}$ in Equation (6) unambiguous.

2. **Asymmetry Between Leave-One-Contract-Out Rescue Credit and Proportional Displacement Cost-Sharing Under Multi-Hold Contention, and Sequential Same-Interval Arrivals (Section IV-B, Section V-B, and Proposition 1, Pages 3–5):**
   While the text following Proposition 1 (page 5) notes that the displacement charge $D_j = \frac{b_j}{h_c}\min(h_c, u_c)$ is a budget-balanced proportional cost-sharing rule rather than a leave-one-contract-out marginal counterfactual, combining proportional cost-sharing for $D_j$ with a leave-one-contract-out rule for admission/QoS rescue ($F_c^{\text{cf}} = F_c + \max(0, m - u_c)$) in Equation (6) introduces a potential theoretical asymmetry when multiple holds or simultaneous handovers contend at the same cell ($h_c > b_j$):
   - *Multi-hold rescue vs. displacement attribution:* In the leave-one-out rescue test for each individual hold $m = b_j$, the released $b_j$ RBs must first absorb the *entire* cell's unmet incumbent demand $u_c$ (which resulted from the aggregate active hold $h_c = \sum_j b_j$) before any remainder $\max(0, b_j - u_c)$ contributes to $F_c^{\text{cf}}$. Conversely, the leave-one-out marginal displacement of hold $j$ is $\min(h_c, u_c) - \min(h_c - b_j, u_c) = \max\big(0, \min(b_j, u_c - (h_c - b_j))\big)$, whereas proportional sharing charges $\frac{b_j}{h_c}\min(h_c, u_c)$. For example, if $C = 60$, incumbent demand is $55$, and two arriving UEs with $d = f = 5$ each hold $b_1 = b_2 = 5\text{ RBs}$ ($h_c = 10, F_c = 0, u_c = 5$), each contract is charged only half of the displacement ($2.5\text{ RBs}$, whereas its leave-one-out marginal displacement is $0$), yet in each UE's leave-one-out rescue test all $5$ released RBs are absorbed by $u_c = 5$ ($F_c^{\text{cf}} = 0 + \max(0, 5 - 5) = 0$), allowing both contracts to claim full admission rescue credit ($\mathbf{1}_1^{\text{adm}} = \mathbf{1}_2^{\text{adm}} = 1$) even though removing both holds simultaneously would have satisfied the $5\text{ RBs}$ of unmet incumbent demand and still left $5$ free RBs to admit the higher-priority arrival without a reservation. Additionally, in Section V-B (page 4), the prose states that a hold *"is charged exactly the service it took,"* which holds at the aggregate cell level (Proposition 1) rather than the individual contract level when $|\mathcal{A}_c(t)| > 1$.
   - *Sequential free-capacity updates across simultaneous handovers:* Section IV-B specifies that *"Handovers into the same cell in one interval are processed in slice-priority order, ties broken by UE identifier,"* yet Equation (1) ($\min(d, m + F_c) \ge f$) and $F_c^{\text{cf}} = F_c + \max(0, m - u_c)$ write $F_c$ as a single static cell quantity for the interval. When multiple handovers arrive at cell $c$ in the same interval, an earlier-processed higher-priority handover can consume unreserved free RBs (specifically $\max(0, \min(d - m, F_c))$) or leave unused RBs from its own hold ($\max(0, m - d)$), and in a lower-priority UE's counterfactual, RBs released by removing its hold could interact with a preceding higher-priority arrival that had unmet arrival demand ($d > m + F_c$). Clarifying how $F_c$ and $F_c^{\text{cf}}$ evolve across sequential same-interval arrivals and discussing the interaction between marginal rescue credit and proportional cost-sharing would tighten Sections IV-B and V.

3. **Specification of Contract Activation Ordering, Pre-Action Capacity Indexing, Post-Rejection Lifecycle, and Retiming Bounds (Sections IV-C and V-C, Equations 9–12, Pages 3–4):**
   Several boundary mechanics governing reservation contract transitions in Sections IV-C and V-C are underspecified:
   - *Pre-activation/pre-request indexing and tie-breaking in Constraints (9) and (10):* Section V-C defines $H_c(t) = \sum_{j \in \mathcal{A}_c(t)} b_j$ and $P_c(t) = \sum_{j \in \mathcal{P}_c(t)} b_j$ as the total RBs of active and pending holds at cell $c$, and writes Constraints (9) and (10) as:
     $$b_j \le C - H_c(t_j^a) - \sum_{u \in \mathcal{U}_c(t_j^a)} f_u(t_j^a), \quad \forall j,$$
     $$b_j \le C - H_c(t) - P_c(t) \text{ at request}, \quad \forall j.$$
     For these inequalities not to double-count $b_j$, $H_c(t_j^a)$ and $P_c(t)$ on the right-hand side must denote the active and pending holds *prior* to activating or committing contract $j$. Moreover, while the text explicitly defines sequential processing and tie-breaking orders for incumbent scheduling (Section IV-B), simultaneous handovers (Section IV-B), and request-time action masking in Constraint (10) (UE-identifier order, Section V-C), it does not specify the evaluation order when multiple pending contracts at the same target cell reach their activation time $t_j^a$ in the same interval and contend for remaining floor-protected capacity under Constraint (9). It is also worth clarifying that floor protection in Constraint (9) is enforced solely at the activation instant $t_j^a$ without preempting already-active holds if incumbent slice floors $\sum_{u \in \mathcal{U}_c(t)} f_u(t)$ rise in later intervals $t > t_j^a$.
   - *Contract lifecycle after activation rejection:* Section IV-C states that if a hold violates Constraint (9) at activation, *"the activation is rejected,"* and concludes the paragraph by stating: *"A contract ends when its UE arrives (settled if the target is right, wrong-target otherwise), when it expires at the forecast horizon, or when it is censored at the end of the run."* Because Constraint (12) permits at most one open contract per UE, it should be clarified whether a rejected activation immediately terminates the contract with reward $R_j = 0$ (freeing the UE to open new decision points in subsequent intervals) or leaves the rejected contract open/inactive until arrival or expiry.
   - *Retiming bounds relative to preparation floor and expiry (Constraint 11):* Section IV-C specifies that while a contract requested at $t_0$ is pending at a later interval $t' > t_0$ with updated lead $k_{i,t'}$ for the same target, *"the delay of the chosen timing mode is recomputed relative to $t'$, the activation never precedes the end of preparation, and the original expiry $t_j^e$ is kept."* First, if the recomputed delay $\delta' \in \{0, \lfloor k_{i,t'}/2 \rfloor, k_{i,t'} - 1\}$ yields $t' + \delta' > t_j^e = t_0 + 8$ (e.g., at $t' = t_0 + 3$ with updated lead $k_{i,t'} = 7$ under `jit` mode, giving $t' + 6 = t_0 + 9 > t_j^e$), it would violate Constraint (11) ($t_0 + \delta_p \le t_j^a \le t_j^e$) unless $t_j^a$ is explicitly clamped from above at $t_j^e$. Second, because the simulator runs `retime` before `activate` in each interval (Algorithm 1, line 11), recomputing $t_j^a$ at $t' > t_0$ as $t' + \max(\delta', \delta_p)$ rather than anchoring the preparation floor at the original request time $t_0 + \delta_p$ would perpetually push a `now` ($\delta' = 0$) contract forward to $t' + 1$. Providing the explicit retiming formula—such as $t_j^a \leftarrow \min(t_j^e, \max(t_0 + \delta_p, t' + \delta'))$—would prevent misinterpretation.

4. **Timeline Alignment Between Figure 1, Equation (5), and Within-Interval Execution Order (Section III, Section IV-C, and Section V-A, Pages 3–4):**
   There is a potential off-by-one indexing ambiguity regarding when a forecast arrival occurs relative to decision interval $t$:
   - Figure 1 (page 3) depicts a decision at $t$, preparation completing at $t + 1$, a hold activating at $t^a$, and the UE arriving at $t + k$.    - In the simulator execution loop (Algorithm 1, page 6, lines 6–11), however, decisions are taken at interval $t$ with $t_j^a = t + \max(\delta, \delta_p)$, and the simulator immediately advances (`retime, activate, schedule, move, admit, settle`) within the same loop iteration $t$. Under that ordering, the first of the $k_{i,t}$ forecast steps occurs in interval $t$ and the $k_{i,t}$-th step occurs in interval $t + k_{i,t} - 1$, which makes `jit` ($\delta = k_{i,t} - 1$) activate during the arrival interval $t + k_{i,t} - 1$ itself and explains why a lead of $k_{i,t} = 1$ arrives before $t + \delta_p = t + 1$ (consistent with the Reactive baseline condition in Section VII-B).
   - Clarifying the exact within-interval event sequence in Section IV-C/V-A to align Figure 1 with Equation (5) would make the exact duration of active holds unambiguous.

5. **Overloaded Notation $D_j$ for Cumulative Contract Displacement versus Single-Interval Displacement (Section V-B and Proposition 1, Pages 4–5):**
   In Section V-B (Equation 6, page 4), $D_j$ is defined at the contract level as the cumulative incumbent RB-intervals displaced by contract $j$ over its entire active duration. 2. **Unbounded Replay Buffer Retention Across $\epsilon$-Annealing and Single-Replica Training-Time Maximization Bias in FQI (Section VI-C, Algorithm 1, Algorithm 2, Section VIII-B–D, and Section IX)**
   - **Location:** Page 5, Section VI-C, Paragraphs 1–2; Page 6, Algorithm 1 (Lines 1, 3, 13–18) and Algorithm 2 (Line 4); Pages 7–9, Section VIII-B–D; and Page 10, Section IX.
   - **Observation:** Across all evaluated displacement prices $\lambda \in \{0.25, 1, 4, 16\}$ (Tables V and VI), `CARE` closely tracks, but consistently trails, the calibrated threshold rules (`Tuned rule` and `Slice-tuned rule`) by $0.05$ to $0.59$ RB per interval. Most notably, at $\lambda = 16$—where optimal utility requires never holding ($0\%$ holds, $U_{16} = 0.00$), which both tuned rules recover—`CARE` still commits holds on $2.0\%$ of decisions and incurs a statistically significant negative utility ($U_{16} = -0.25$, Holm-adjusted $p = 0.00824$). Meanwhile, the single-replica ablation (`CARE single network`) exhibits severe overestimation bias, jumping from $26\%$ to $65\%$ holds at $\lambda = 1$ and from $24\%$ to $49\%$ at $\lambda = 0.25$, while degrading to $U_{16} = -4.11$ at $\lambda = 16$. The authors note two key properties of their learning setup: (i) taking a greedy maximum over nine noisy hold values favors whichever value is overestimated, which they address solely via inference-time averaging over three independently trained Q-network replicas (Algorithm 2, Line 4) because *"it leaves the stopping targets unchanged"*; and (ii) full replay-buffer reuse across all 240 episodes introduces cross-UE non-stationarity because $\epsilon$ anneals from $1.0$ to $0.05$ over the first 180 episodes, so early episodes are hold-heavy and reflect higher contention than later episodes.
   - **Potential Concern:** Two interactions in Algorithm 1 represent methodological concerns that directly contribute to the residual value-estimation noise noted in Section IX, the slight underperformance of `CARE` relative to tuned threshold rules, and its $2.0\%$ hold rate at $\lambda = 16$:
     - *Propagated Training-Time Overestimation Bias vs. Inference-Only Ensemble Averaging:* Committed `hold` actions regress directly onto realized counterfactual returns $y = R_j$ without bootstrapping, making their targets unbiased conditional on the rollout policy. In contrast, in Algorithm 1 (Line 16), each replica $Q_{\theta_m}$ is trained completely independently using single-network maximization over the noisy actions for its bootstrap wait target, $y = \gamma \max\big(0, \max_{x'} Q_{\bar{\theta}}(s', x')\big)$. By Jensen's inequality, zero-mean estimation noise across the discrete actions at $s'$ yields $\mathbb{E}[\max_{x'} Q_{\bar{\theta}}(s', x')] \ge \max_{x'} \mathbb{E}[Q_{\bar{\theta}}(s', x')]$, preserving positive estimation noise above the $\max(0, \cdot)$ floor and iteratively propagating upward bias backward into $Q_\theta(s, \text{WAIT})$ throughout FQI training. At $\lambda = 0.25$, upward bias in multi-step wait targets can make waiting appear overly attractive relative to immediate holding ($23.7\%$ holds for `CARE` vs. $100\%$ for the tuned rule), whereas at $\lambda = 16$, averaging three independently biased replicas only at test time reduces variance by $1/\sqrt{3}$ without removing systematic positive bias already baked into each network's Q-values. Evaluating a training-time bias-reduction ablation in Algorithm 1—such as Double-FQI targets (decoupling action selection and value evaluation across replicas during Line 16) or computing the target backup $\max_{x'} \bar{Q}(s', x')$ using the ensemble mean—would clarify whether `CARE`'s slight deficit relative to the threshold rules is an inherent sample-complexity limitation over 240 episodes or an artifact of uncorrected training-time maximization bias.
     - *Unbounded Replay Buffer Retention Under High Early Contention:* Because 9 of the 10 actions commit a hold, near-uniform exploration during the first 180 episodes causes most forecasted UEs to immediately commit a contract. Because $\mathcal{D}$ accumulates all transitions from $e = 0$ to $239$ without sliding-window eviction or recency weighting, and Line 17 fits $Q_\theta$ uniformly across $\mathcal{D}$, committed contracts (`mid` or `jit`) logged in early episodes settle up to 8 intervals later under a surge of competing random holds arriving between decision time $t$ and arrival time $t+k$ that cannot be predicted from the causal state $s$ at time $t$. Since terminal hold targets $y = R_j$ are never recomputed as the policy improves, retaining early high-$\epsilon$ episodes indefinitely anchors $Q_\theta(s, x)$ to an artificial contention regime (distorting both counterfactual rescues $1_j^{\text{adm}}$, displacement charges $\lambda D_j$, and successor legal-action masks $M'$) relative to the low-contention regime near $\epsilon = 0.05$.

#### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

1. **Confounding of Action-Space Parameterization, Return Estimation, Terminology, and Evaluation Parity in the PPO Diagnostic Ablations (Section VI-D, Tables III, V, and VI, Figs. 6–7, Section VIII-D, and Section IX):**
   * **Potential Concern:** Section VI-D (p. 5), Section VIII-D (*Learner*, p. 9), and Section IX (p. 10) diagnose why policy-gradient training (`CARE-PPO` and `CARE-PPO w/ on-policy wait`) collapses into all-or-nothing policies ($100\%$ or $0\%$ holds) on this optimal-stopping formulation. Specifically, Section VI-D states that under on-policy rollouts, because 9 of the 10 actions are holds, a near-uniform initial policy almost always holds within the next few decisions so waiting and holding receive nearly the same return, and adds: *"Flooring the sampled continuation at 0 removes the collapse but overestimates waiting, because a single lucky rescue dominates the sampled maximum. Both PPO variants are reported as ablations... both settle into all-or-nothing policies early in training."* Five mathematical and experimental aspects of this analysis warrant sharper precision:
     1. *Terminology ("removes the collapse") vs. All-or-Nothing Behavior:* Stating in Section VI-D that flooring the sampled continuation at 0 *"removes the collapse"* conflicts with the immediately following sentence (*"both settle into all-or-nothing policies"*) and with Table VI and Figs. 6–7, which show that `CARE-PPO` still collapses to $100\%$ holds at $\lambda \in \{0.25, 1\}$ and collapses to $0\%$ holds (always waiting) at $\lambda \in \{4, 16\}$. It would be more precise to state that zero-flooring prevents the unconditional *all-hold* collapse at high prices ($\lambda \ge 4$), shifting it instead to an *all-wait* collapse.
     2. *Sample-Max Bias in Floored Monte Carlo Returns vs. "Sampled Maximum":* In a Monte Carlo rollout following a `WAIT` decision, the trajectory yields a single realized return $G_{t+1}$ rather than a maximum over multiple candidate actions. If `CARE-PPO` defines the return of waiting as $\gamma \max(0, G_{t+1})$, this does not estimate the Bellman optimal-stopping target $\gamma \max(0, \max_{x'} \mathbb{E}[R_j \mid s', x'])$ in Eq. (14). Instead, placing the convex operator $\max(0, \cdot)$ inside the expectation computes $\mathbb{E}[\max(0, G_{t+1})] \ge \max(0, \mathbb{E}[G_{t+1}])$ by Jensen's inequality, truncating negative displacement realizations ($R_j < 0$) to zero on a per-sample basis while preserving positive realizations ("lucky rescues"), whereas bootstrapping the `WAIT` continuation from the learned state-value critic floored at zero ($\gamma \max(0, V_\phi(s'))$) avoids single-sample max bias. Explicitly stating the return/advantage equations used for both PPO variants will make this mechanism unambiguous.
     3. *Flat Action-Space Prior Skew:* Because 9 of the 10 discrete actions in the unfactored categorical head are holds and only 1 is `WAIT`, a near-uniform initial policy assigns a $90\%$ prior probability to holding at every decision step (meaning an on-policy trajectory survives $k$ steps without holding to observe the zero return of never holding with probability $0.1^k$, e.g., $10^{-8}$ at $k=8$). Moreover, at $\lambda = 16$, holding early (`now` at lead $k$) incurs more active intervals of displacement penalty $\lambda D_j$ than waiting several steps before holding, yet `CARE-PPO w/ on-policy wait` still collapses to $100\%$ holds (`floor/jit`). In optimal-stopping formulations with factored action spaces—specifically $\{\text{WAIT}\} \cup (\mathcal{B}_{i,t} \times \mathcal{T}_{i,t})$—standard policy-gradient implementations either use a hierarchical two-stage policy head (a binary `WAIT` vs. `HOLD` stopping decision initialized at $0.5/0.5$, paired with a conditional 9-way distribution over amount and timing) or initialize the `WAIT` logit bias to $+\ln 9$ to isolate credit assignment from the $9:1$ initialization prior.
     4. *Unexplained Numerical Identity with `Floor-JIT`:* In Table V ($\lambda = 1$) and Table VI ($\lambda \in \{0.25, 1, 16\}$), whenever the PPO variants commit $100\%$ holds, every reported metric (`HSR = 87.8`, `QoS = 50.9`, `Outage = 1.38`, `Full = 74.2`, `Served = 80.4`, `Displ. = 0.87`, `U1 = 12.56`, `U0.25 = 14.12`, `U16 = -18.73`) matches `Floor-JIT` to every decimal place. While the text explains why PPO never chooses `WAIT`, it does not explicitly state why the actor converges deterministically to the single `floor/jit` action out of the nine candidate hold actions ($\{\hat{f}, \hat{d}, \hat{d}^+\} \times \{\text{now}, \text{mid}, \text{jit}\}$) on $100\%$ of test decisions.
     5. *Incomplete Price Sweep and Episode Budget Parity:* In Table III (p. 6), the PPO ablations are trained for 160 episodes rather than the 240 episodes used for `CARE`, and in Table VI and Fig. 6 (p. 9), `CARE-PPO w/ on-policy wait` is evaluated only at $\lambda \in \{1, 16\}$, leaving missing entries (`–`) at $\lambda \in \{0.25, 4\}$. Moreover, Table VI shows that `CARE-PPO` (with the floored backup) achieves slightly higher mean test utility than `CARE` at three of the four prices ($\lambda = 0.25$: $14.12$ vs. $14.10$; $\lambda = 1$: $12.56$ vs. $12.38$; $\lambda = 16$: $0.00$ vs. $-0.25$), underperforming `CARE` only at $\lambda = 4$ ($0.00$ vs. $6.45$).
   * **Suggested Improvement:** Clarifying in Sections VI-D, VIII-D, and IX the exact return/advantage equations, noting that the observed collapse is specific to an unfactored 10-way softmax parameterization without prior balancing (or evaluating a factored binary stopping head across all four prices at the full 240-episode budget), and explicitly stating in Section VIII-D that the holding PPO policies converge deterministically to the `floor/jit` action (which minimizes displacement $\lambda D_j$ while meeting the admission floor), will make the diagnostic comparison more rigorous and prevent readers from mistaking the identical rows in Tables V and VI for a tabulation error.
